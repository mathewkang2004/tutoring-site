Unit 2: Rhetoric & Argumentative Writing

Key Concepts

The rhetorical appeals

  • Ethos: appeals to credibility/character (“As a doctor with 20 years of experience…”)
  • Pathos: appeals to emotion (vivid stories, emotionally charged language)
  • Logos: appeals to logic (statistics, reasoning, cause-and-effect)

Strong arguments usually blend all three rather than relying on just one.

Rhetorical situation

Before analyzing or writing an argument, identify:

  • Author — who is speaking, and what’s their credibility/bias?
  • Audience — who is this trying to persuade, and what do they already believe?
  • Purpose — what does the author want the audience to think or do?
  • Context — what’s happening around this text that shapes it?

Building an argumentative essay

  1. Thesis: a clear, arguable claim (not a fact, not a question) — should be specific enough that someone could reasonably disagree.
  2. Body paragraphs: each should have one main point (topic sentence), evidence, and analysis that ties back to the thesis.
  3. Counterargument: acknowledge the strongest opposing view, then rebut it — this makes your argument look stronger, not weaker.
  4. Conclusion: don’t just restate the thesis — address the “so what?” (why does this argument matter?).
TipTesting a thesis

Ask: “could a reasonable person disagree with this?” If not, it’s a fact, not an arguable thesis. “Recycling reduces landfill waste” is a fact. “Mandatory recycling programs are more effective than voluntary ones” is arguable.

Practice Problems

  1. Identify the appeal: “Over 10,000 peer-reviewed studies confirm this treatment’s effectiveness.”
NoteSolution

Logos — it uses statistics/data as logical evidence.

  1. Rewrite this weak thesis into an arguable one: “Social media has both good and bad effects on teenagers.”
NoteSolution

Sample fix: “While social media offers useful ways for teenagers to connect, its design features (infinite scroll, algorithmic feeds) create outsized harm to teen mental health that outweighs its benefits.” (Key fix: pick a side and make a specific, debatable claim instead of listing both sides neutrally.)

  1. Why should you include a counterargument in your own essay instead of only presenting your own side?
NoteSolution

Addressing the strongest opposing view — and then rebutting it — shows you’ve considered the issue fully, which makes your own argument more credible and harder to dismiss. Ignoring counterarguments can make your essay look one-sided or unaware of obvious objections.
